        Sources and method

        This is an independent, unofficial reconstruction of the final flight of Learjet 60 N722BD on 1 November 2026. Nine people on board died. The NTSB investigation is ongoing and its findings take precedence over anything shown here.

        What is measured

        • Position, altitude, groundspeed and track are the aircraft's own ADS-B broadcasts, from the adsb.lol open archive (Open Database License).
        • Temperature, pressure and wind come from the two Miami weather-balloon soundings that bracket the accident (12:00 UTC 1 Nov and 00:00 UTC 2 Nov), via the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive.
        • Speed and load limits are from the Learjet 60 Airplane Flight Manual.

        What is computed or estimated

        • Descent rate in the dive is computed from GPS altitude, because the broadcast rate stops at −32,640 ft/min.
        • Mach and calibrated airspeed are computed from groundspeed, descent rate and the measured atmosphere. Expect about ±0.03 Mach and ±5% airspeed.
        • Bank and load are computed from how fast the track and path angle change. The aircraft's attitude is drawn along its flight path; the real pitch and roll attitude were not broadcast.
        • The radio captions are a machine transcript of a 116-second air-traffic-control clip, tidied by hand; some words are uncertain and marked. The clip has no clock. It is placed against the track by matching the pilot's descent callouts ("ten thousand feet per minute", "overspeed condition ... twenty thousand feet per minute") to the descent in the ADS-B data, which puts the start of the clip at about 17:47:29 UTC, good to about ±4 seconds. The recording itself is not part of this site; you can load your own copy to hear it in step with the flight. What the pilots said to each other in the cockpit is not public and is not shown.
        • Pressure altitude stopped updating at 8,750 ft; below that only GPS altitude is shown.
        • The NTSB reported most of the left elevator in three pieces, 1.3 to 3.8 miles from the impact site. When each piece left the aircraft is not known. The model shows the elevator as "separating" along the stretch of track that lies 3.8 to 1.3 miles out; that timing is an estimate.
        • The drawn path is lightly smoothed during the dive, because GPS altitude is broadcast in 25 ft steps and positions carry small timing errors. The readouts use the unsmoothed values.
        • Weather notes are from public archives: Convective SIGMET bulletins from the NWS Aviation Weather Center, airport weather reports (METARs) and pilot reports, via the Iowa Environmental Mesonet. Whether the jet was inside a SIGMET area was checked by plotting the bulletin's reference points against the track; the Miami reference point is approximate.
        • Weather radar is from the Miami (KAMX) NEXRAD radar, Level II data from NOAA, volume scans starting 17:40:32, 17:45:48 and 17:51:04 UTC. The radar is about 120 km from the dive, where its beam is about 2 km wide and the tilts are about 2 km apart, so heights are coarse. A full scan takes about five minutes: the tilts that reach cruise level passed the place where the dive began at 17:49:29, 73 seconds after the jet was there. Each point along the path uses the two scans nearest in time. Radar shows rain and ice particles; it does not measure turbulence or icing directly.
        • The street-level camera position is approximate.
